# Copywriting guide

> The measurable half of this is in spec sections 6-10-4 and 7-2. This document is
> the writing half: how to produce copy that clears those thresholds rather than
> fights them.
>
> **Everything here is derived from four state pages that were written and then
> measured** (spec 6-10-4-1), not from general advice. Where a number appears, it
> is what those four actually did.

---

## 1. The one rule everything else serves

> **A page must contain at least one section that would be factually wrong or
> meaningless if you swapped the entity name.**

This is stricter than the 40% uniqueness score and it is the real standard. A page
can pass the score and still be a doorway page; it cannot pass this test and be
one.

Ask before publishing: *if I replaced every instance of "Maryland" with "Virginia",
what breaks?* If the answer is "nothing but the name", the page is not finished.

---

## 2. Measured targets

From the four pilot bodies, which scored 46–47% unique against each other:

| Metric | Pilot actual | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Words | 790–848 | **900–1,100** — see the length finding below |
| Sentences | 23–24 | scale with length |
| Mean sentence length | 22.6 words | **20–24** |
| Sentences under 12 words | 15% | **10–20%** |
| Sentences over 30 words | 16% | **under 20%** |
| Paragraphs | 11–13 | 3–5 sentences each |
| H2 sections | 4 | 4–6 |
| Entity-specific numbers | 3–29 | **≥3**, more where the data allows |

**The rhythm matters more than the average.** A page of uniform 22-word sentences
reads like a machine wrote it. The pilot's 15% short and 16% long is what makes
the average readable — a four-word sentence after a thirty-word one is what a
human does.

### The length finding — measured, and it changes the target

The same four bodies were measured twice, before and after being grown:

| Length | Median unique | Worst |
|---|---|---|
| ~530 words | 47% | 46% |
| ~800 words | 45% | **43%** |

**Uniqueness fell as length rose**, and at 800 words the worst page sits exactly on
the 43% requirement with nothing spare. The added 270 words were genuine
state-specific material — county spreads, the franchise tax, the Net Profits Tax —
and the ratio still slipped, because every additional paragraph also adds shared
vocabulary: *rate*, *income*, *state*, *resident*, *federal*.

The conclusion is not to write less. It is that **length must be earned**:

> **900 words of dense entity-specific material beats 1,400 words where the last
> 500 are general.** Spec 7-2 permits up to 1,400; the measurement says treat the
> bottom of that range as the target and go higher only when the entity genuinely
> has more of its own to say.

A page approaching 1,400 words should be checked, not assumed. Run the validator
before adding the last section, not after.

> Texas has only 3 numbers where Maryland has 29. That is correct: a state with no
> income tax has fewer figures to state. **Do not manufacture numbers to hit a
> count** — find the state's own facts, and if there are three, write three.

## 3. The opening

**Every page opens by naming the entity's structural characteristic in the first
clause.** Not with a greeting, not with the reader, not with a definition.

What the pilot did:

| State | Opening |
|---|---|
| New York | "New York runs one of the most steeply graduated income tax schedules in the country." |
| Pennsylvania | "Pennsylvania taxes income at a flat 3.07%, one of the lowest state rates in the country — and that headline figure is misleading in two specific ways." |
| Texas | "Texas levies no broad-based individual income tax on earned income." |
| Maryland | "Maryland's schedule looks graduated on paper and behaves almost like a flat tax in practice." |

Each states a fact that is true of that state and false of most others, in the
first fifteen words. Three of the four then set up a tension the page resolves —
"misleading in two ways", "looks graduated but behaves flat" — which is what makes
the reader continue.

**Never open with any of these:**

- "If you live in {State}, you may be wondering…"
- "{State} income tax can be confusing."
- "Understanding {State} income tax is important for…"
- "Whether you are a freelancer or an employee…"
- Any sentence that would be equally true of all 51 jurisdictions.

The last is the test. **If the opening sentence works on every page, it belongs on
none of them.**

## 4. Section playbook

### "How {State} income tax works"

Give the structure, then say **what the structure actually means for a normal
earner**. The second half is what competitors omit.

- Progressive: name the brackets, then identify **which one governs an ordinary
  salary**. New York has nine brackets; the useful sentence is that the first four
  are exhausted within a fortnight and 5.9% is where most professional income sits.
- Flat: name the rate, then name **what the flat rate conceals** — usually the
  absence of a deduction, or a local layer.
- No tax: state it in one sentence and move immediately to what replaces it. Do
  not pad an absence.

### "Local tax in {State}"

Usually the highest-value section on the page, because national calculators skip
it. Say precisely how far it reaches:

- **"Every county levies"** (Maryland) is a categorically different statement from
  **"most municipalities levy"** (Pennsylvania) or **"two cities levy"** (New York).
  Get the quantifier right; it is the whole meaning of the section.
- If the state genuinely has none, say so in one sentence and do not inflate it.

### "Filing and deadlines in {State}"

Name the actual authority — *the Comptroller of Maryland*, *the Department of
Taxation and Finance*. Naming the body is a small precision signal that generic
content never has, and it is a fact that cannot survive a find-and-replace.

### "Who this suits, and who it does not"

**This section must make an argument, not a summary.** It is the one most likely
to collapse into filler.

Each pilot page reaches a genuinely different conclusion:

| State | The argument |
|---|---|
| New York | Gentle at low income, and the city line changes everything |
| Pennsylvania | Attractive at high income, punishing at low; **municipality decides, not state** |
| Texas | Depends on whether you own property — the composition changes, not just the size |
| Maryland | Unremarkable at the state layer, distinctive at the county one |

If your conclusion would fit another state, you have not found the argument yet.

## 5. Constructions to avoid

| Do not write | Why | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| "It is important to note that…" | Says nothing | Delete it; state the thing |
| "There are many factors to consider…" | Filler that promises without delivering | Name the factors |
| "In conclusion" / "To summarise" | The reader can see the page ending | Delete |
| "very", "extremely", "significantly" | Intensifiers substitute for evidence | Give the number |
| "This will save you money" | A promise we cannot make | "This changes the figure by $X" |
| "Everyone knows" / "Obviously" | Condescending, and often false | Delete |
| Rhetorical questions as headings | Filler dressed as structure | State the answer as the heading |
| The same sentence structure three times running | Reads as generated | Vary it |

**On hedging (YMYL):** write "this may apply" rather than "this will apply" where
the outcome depends on circumstances we do not know. That is both honest and
legally sound. But **do not hedge a fact**: Pennsylvania's rate is 3.07%, not
"approximately 3.07%".

---

## 6. Numbers

- Percentages as the source states them: **3.07%**, not "about 3%".
- Dollars with thousands separators and no cents in prose: **$95,000**, and
  **$74,160** rather than $74,159.76 in body copy. The calculator gives the cent;
  the sentence gives the figure.
- **Every number traceable to the dataset or a primary source.** If you cannot
  point at where it came from, it does not go in.
- Bracket thresholds exactly: **$215,400**, never "around $215,000".
- Give a number its meaning in the same sentence: "$7,167 — 7.5% of gross income"
  says something that "$7,167" alone does not.

## 7. Finding the angle — the research method

Before writing, read that entity's dataset row and ask four questions:

1. **What is unusual about its structure?** Ten brackets that behave like one.
   A flat rate with no deduction. Nine brackets exhausted in a fortnight.
2. **What does a national calculator get wrong here?** Almost always the local
   layer. This is reliably the most valuable section.
3. **What would surprise someone who only read the headline rate?** Pennsylvania's
   3.07% looks like the lowest in the country until the local earned income tax
   and Philadelphia's Net Profits Tax enter.
4. **Who is this genuinely good for, and who is it bad for?** If the answer is the
   same as the last state you wrote, look harder.

**If all four answers are dull, the page may not deserve to exist.** That is a
legitimate finding — spec 6-10-3's gate exists precisely so that pages without
substance are not built.

- [ ] The find-and-replace test (section 1)

- [ ] At least one section a competitor's page could not contain
- [ ] The authority is named, not "the state tax department"
- [ ] Every number traceable to the dataset or a primary source
- [ ] No banned construction from section 5
