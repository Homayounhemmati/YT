The federal minimum wage has been $7.25 an hour since July 2009. At 2,080 hours a year
that is $15,080 before tax, which is below the poverty guideline for a household of two
in every state. The distance between a legal wage and a wage you can actually live on
is the entire subject of this page.

## A living wage is computed, not legislated

A minimum wage is a floor set by statute. A living wage is an arithmetic result: add up
what one specific place charges for housing, food, transport, health care and taxes for
one specific household, then solve backwards for the gross salary that covers it.

```
metro rent (HUD)        +
goods and services (BEA) +
health care and transport +
federal, state and FICA tax
= the gross salary this place requires
```

Because the inputs are local, so is the answer. The same household composition produces
figures that differ by a factor of two between the cheapest and most expensive US
metros, and the ranking is not the one most people would guess — several high-wage
metros leave a household worse off than lower-wage ones once rent is counted.

## Why the tax layer cannot be skipped

Costs are paid out of net income, but salaries are quoted in gross. A tool that
compares a metro's costs against a gross figure is comparing two things measured on
different scales. Nine states levy no income tax at all; the rest range from a flat
rate near 3% to progressive schedules passing 10%. On a single $95,000 salary the
spread between the best and worst state runs to roughly $7,200 a year, which is a
meaningful share of what a household would call breathing room.

This is why **how much do i need to earn to live in** a given city is a tax question as
much as a rent question, and why a figure quoted without the state attached is not
usable.

## Household size changes the answer more than the city does

One adult, two adults with one earner, and two adults with two children produce three
different answers in the same metro, and the gaps between them are usually larger than
the gaps between metros. Child care in particular behaves less like a budget line and
more like a second rent: in much of the country it exceeds the cost of housing for a
household with two young children.

## What this figure is and is not

It is a break-even, not a target. It covers costs without debt repayment, without
retirement saving beyond payroll deductions, and without any discretionary spending at
all. A **salary needed to live** figure that included savings would be a different and
larger number, and it is worth knowing how far above the floor an offer actually sits
before treating it as comfortable.
