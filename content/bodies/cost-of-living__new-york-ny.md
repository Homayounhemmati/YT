New York is the one American city where rent alone is more than half of a single
person's month. On the federal government's own price measures, the New York metro
area sits 12.6 points above the national average overall, and housing rents sit 48.6
points above it — so the **cost of living in New York** is, first and last, the cost of
a lease.

## Manhattan-level prices, month by month

A single New Yorker renting a one-bedroom at HUD's benchmark of $2,655, and otherwise
spending like a typical one-person household at New York prices, runs through about
$5,068 a month.

Twelve such months come to $60,816, with 52 cents of every dollar going to rent
and utilities. Add a partner and two children, move to a two-bedroom at $2,910, and the
month grows to about $8,790 while rent's share drops to 33%: groceries, doctors and
transport are what grow, not the lease.

The budget's two car lines — purchase and fuel, then insurance and repairs — show how an
average can mislead about the cost to live in New York: they are a national household's
driving, priced at New York's level. Many New Yorkers own no car at all, and for them
those lines largely disappear while the fares line grows; the cost of living calculator
lets you replace each with your own figure.

## One rent for five boroughs

HUD sets a single Fair Market Rent for Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx and
Staten Island: $2,529 for a studio, $2,655 for one bedroom, $3,644 for three. It is the
rent at the 40th percentile of what recent movers paid across the whole area, including
the utilities a tenant pays. That makes it a fair typical figure for the city and a low
one for much of Manhattan, where asking rents run well above it. **Average rent in New
York** depends more on the neighbourhood than in any other city on this site, and the
benchmark is best read as a floor for the core and a ceiling for the outer boroughs.

## What the other indices say

Utilities stand at 127.0 against the national 100, goods at 110.3 and other services —
restaurants, health care, personal services — at 105.8. Every component is above
average, which is what sets New York apart from cities such as Austin or Tampa, where
one expensive component sits beside several cheap ones. Here there is no cheaper line
to lean on.

## New York tax, and the city's own

On a $95,000 salary a single New York State resident keeps about $70,656 after federal
tax, Social Security, Medicare, $4,565 of state tax and the state's paid family leave
and disability contributions — an effective 25.2%. That is before New York City's own
resident income tax, which applies on top for anyone living in the five boroughs and is
not included in any figure on this page.

A household living in Westchester or on Long Island and commuting in pays the state
tax but not the city's, which is a large part of why so many do.

## When New York makes sense

- **Households that can use the city without a car.** Take the two car lines out and
  New York's month shrinks by several hundred dollars; keep them and it is one of the
  most expensive months anywhere in the country.
- **Families more than single renters, relative to income.** The extra bedroom costs
  comparatively little here, so the family's rent share is far below the single
  renter's.
- **Salaries that rise with the city's.** The prices are above average in every
  category, so a New York salary has to be too; an offer that matches a national
  figure is, in practice, a pay cut.

The hardest case is a solo renter below 96th Street on a median paycheck: more than
half the month to the landlord, then the city tax on what remains.
