Florida is one of nine states that tax no wages at all, and a Florida paycheck shows
it: the only deductions are federal income tax, Social Security and Medicare. On the
same gross pay, that leaves a Florida employee more than someone earning identically
in any state that levies an income tax.

## The numbers on a $95,000 salary

A single filer with no pre-tax deductions keeps **$75,663 a year** — an effective rate
of 20.4%. The federal layer takes $12,070 and Social Security plus Medicare take
$7,268. There is no third line.

Paid every two weeks, that is about $2,910 a check. Paid twice a month, about $3,153.
Monthly payroll, which some Florida employers in education and government still use,
comes to about $6,305.

A **florida take home pay calculator** that shows a larger figure than this for the
same salary has either left out FICA or assumed pre-tax deductions you have not
entered; one that shows less has usually applied self-employment tax.

## Moving to Florida from a taxed state

Most people running these numbers are comparing a job in Florida with one somewhere
else. The state layer is where that comparison moves: on this salary, the gap between
the best and worst states runs to more than $7,500 a year, about 8% of gross, and
Florida sits at the top of that range.

What the paycheck does not show is the rest of the cost of living. Florida funds
itself through sales tax, property tax and tourism levies rather than wages, and home
insurance in coastal counties has become a large recurring cost. A paycheck that is
bigger in Florida is a starting point for the comparison, not the conclusion of it.

## Tipped and hourly work

Florida has a large hospitality workforce, and hourly and tipped pay run through the
same federal deductions. Tips reported to the employer are wages for Social Security,
Medicare and income tax withholding, even when they arrive in cash. A **florida
paycheck tax calculator** that only takes a base hourly rate will understate the tax
on a tipped job for exactly that reason.

## If you are self-employed

The federal side is heavier. The same $95,000 as self-employment profit carries
$20,840 of tax rather than $19,338, leaving $74,160 — still with no state line, but
with both halves of Social Security and Medicare falling on one person.

## Not included here

- Pre-tax 401(k) contributions, which lower income tax but not Social Security or
  Medicare.
- Health, dental and vision premiums taken through payroll, which lower all three.
- Additional Medicare Tax above $200,000 of wages for a single filer.
