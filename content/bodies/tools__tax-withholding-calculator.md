A refund feels like a win and is usually a mistake. It means you lent the
government money at zero interest for up to sixteen months. A bill feels like a
loss and is usually fine, unless it is large enough to carry a penalty. The point
of withholding is to land near neither.

## How withholding is calculated

A **federal withholding calculator** works backwards from the tax you will owe to the amount that should be coming out of each check.

Your employer does not know your tax situation. They know what is on your **Form
W-4**, and they apply the IRS withholding tables to it each pay period.

The current W-4 has no allowances — those were removed in 2020, and any advice
mentioning "claiming two" predates the form in use. It now asks for:

- **Filing status**, which sets the bracket table used.
- **Multiple jobs or a working spouse** (Step 2), the single most common source of
  under-withholding.
- **Dependents** (Step 3), which reduces withholding by a dollar amount.
- **Other income and deductions** (Step 4), for income with no withholding of its
  own.
- **Extra withholding per period** (Step 4c), a flat addition and the simplest
  correction available.

**Step 2 is where most under-withholding comes from.** Each employer withholds as
though its salary were your only income, so two jobs each withholding correctly
still leaves you short — both applied the lower brackets to their own slice, and
your combined income sits higher.

## Getting to the right answer

```
annual tax owed − withholding to date = remaining shortfall
shortfall ÷ pay periods left = extra per period (Step 4c)
```

**Safe harbour** is the threshold that matters. Penalties are avoided by paying
either 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's — 110% if the prior year's AGI
exceeded $150,000. Meeting either removes the penalty regardless of what is owed
at filing.

**Mid-year is the moment to check.** Six months of payslips give a reliable
projection, and six months remain to correct it a little at a time. The same
correction in December has to come out of two paychecks.

## What this does not include

- State withholding, which most states run on their own separate form.
- Quarterly estimated payments, which cover income with no employer to withhold
  from.
- Tax credits, which reduce what is owed and therefore how much needs withholding.
- Bonuses, withheld at a flat supplemental rate that rarely matches your marginal
  rate.

## Who needs this

Anyone who owed money last April, anyone who received a large refund, anyone who
started a second job, and anyone whose household gained or lost an income. Those
four cover almost every case where **how much should I withhold** has changed since
the W-4 on file was written.

A **W-4 calculator** is not a once-a-lifetime exercise. The form on file reflects
the year it was submitted, and it keeps applying the assumptions of that year until
somebody replaces it.
