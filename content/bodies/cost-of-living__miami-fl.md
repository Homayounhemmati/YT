Miami's housing rents stand higher on the federal price index than New York's — 155.6
against 148.6, where the national average is 100. Everything else in the Miami metro
area is priced much closer to normal, and Florida takes no income tax at all, so the
**cost of living in Miami** is a trade between one of the most expensive rent lines in the
country and take-home pay as high as it gets anywhere.

## Miami-Dade rent at Miami prices

Miami-Dade's one-bedroom benchmark alone is $1,995 — 45% of a single renter's $4,386
month and of a $52,634 year. A couple with two children pays $2,436 for two bedrooms or
$3,127 for three, inside a month of about $8,244.

## Is Miami expensive?

For renters, yes, and more than the overall score of 114.2 suggests, because the score
averages a very expensive rent line with ordinary ones. Goods stand at 103.6, other
services at 109.1, and utilities at 97.2 — slightly below average despite the air
conditioning, because Florida's electricity is cheaper per unit than in the Northeast.

What makes Miami distinctive is how little of the premium is anything but housing. A
household that already owns, or that can hold its rent down, lives in a city whose
everyday prices sit only a few points above the national level.

**Average rent in Miami** at HUD's benchmark runs from $1,828 for a studio to $3,613 for
four bedrooms, for Miami-Dade County, including the utilities a tenant pays. Neighbouring
Broward and Palm Beach counties share the metro area's price level but have their own
Fair Market Rents, and the cost of living calculator prices each of them separately.

## No income tax, and where Florida collects instead

A single person on $95,000 keeps about $75,663 in Florida after federal tax, Social
Security and Medicare — an effective 20.4%, with no state layer and no city income tax.
Against New York the same salary keeps several thousand dollars more a year, which pays
a real share of the rent difference between the two cities, though not all of it.

The state collects instead through sales tax and property tax, and, for anyone who owns
or insures a home, through the cost of insurance. None of it appears on a paycheck; all
of it appears in the monthly budget, and in South Florida home insurance premiums have
risen sharply in recent years.

Miami's break-even salaries are among the highest on this site, almost entirely because
of rent.

## Who Miami's trade favours

- **High earners moving from taxed states.** The higher the salary, the more the
  missing state tax is worth, and at high incomes it covers a large part of Miami's
  rent premium over the national average.
- **Owners rather than renters.** Every non-housing component is close to normal; take
  rent out of the month and Miami is only modestly above average.
- **People who would pay a premium for the climate anyway.** The data prices the rent;
  it does not price the reason people pay it.

The worst fit is a solo renter in Brickell or Wynwood on an ordinary paycheck: nearly
half the month to rent, and a tax saving too small at that income to cover it.
