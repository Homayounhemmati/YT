Michigan taxes wages at a flat 4.25% and allows no standard deduction for workers.
The only thing subtracted before the rate applies is a personal exemption — $5,900 for
2026 — and it is claimed once for every person in the household, which makes the
number of people on the return matter more here than in most flat-tax states.

## $95,000 in Michigan

A single filer subtracts one $5,900 exemption and pays 4.25% on the rest: **$3,787**
of state tax, about 4.0% of the salary. After $12,070 of federal income tax and
$7,268 of Social Security and Medicare, take-home is **$71,876**, an effective 24.3%.

Per check that comes to about $2,764 every two weeks, $2,995 twice a month, $5,990 on
a monthly payroll or $1,382 a week. A **michigan take home pay calculator** that
leaves out the exemption overstates the state line for a single filer, and by four
times as much for a family of four.

## The exemption does the work

Because there is only one rate, the exemption is the whole of Michigan's progressivity.
A married couple claims two, $11,800 between them, and each child or other dependent
adds another $5,900. The amount is indexed to inflation and has climbed steadily —
$5,600 for 2024, $5,800 for 2025, $5,900 for 2026 — so the state tax on an unchanged
salary edges down slightly each year.

Two households on the same $95,000 can therefore owe quite different amounts: the
single filer above, and a couple with two children who subtract four exemptions before
the rate applies. A **michigan income tax calculator** has to ask who is on the return,
not only what the job pays.

## The year the rate moved

Michigan's rate is not quite fixed in law. A revenue trigger written into the statute
cut it to 4.05% for 2023 when state revenue grew fast enough, and the Treasury treated
that cut as good for one year only. The rate went back to 4.25% for 2024 and has stayed
there through 2026. The trigger remains on the books, so a future year could bring
another temporary reduction — a change a calculator should pick up from the Treasury's
annual figures rather than assume.

## City income tax

About two dozen Michigan cities levy an income tax of their own, Detroit, Grand Rapids,
Lansing and Flint among them. Residents pay the city's full rate on all their wages;
people who live elsewhere but work inside the city pay half that rate on what they
earn there. Detroit's rates are the highest in the state, and the city tax is withheld
by the employer on the same paycheck as the state tax.

That layer is not included in the figures above, and it is the main reason two
Michigan paychecks on the same salary differ. Living in Ann Arbor or Troy, which levy
no city tax, and working in Detroit costs the non-resident rate; living in Detroit
costs the full one wherever the job is.

## Form MI-W4 and filing

Withholding runs on Michigan's own certificate, Form MI-W4, which counts exemptions
rather than following the federal W-4's steps. Returns go to the Michigan Department of
Treasury on Form MI-1040, with city returns filed separately — Detroit's is processed by
the state Treasury as well.

## Salaried and hourly pay

An hourly worker's state tax is the same arithmetic on whatever the hours come to:
4.25% above the exemption, with overtime taxed at the same rate as the base. There is
no higher state bracket for a raise or extra hours to reach.

## Self-employed

The same $95,000 as self-employment profit leaves $70,658. The state takes $3,502
rather than $3,787, because half of the self-employment tax comes off federal adjusted
gross income, which is where Michigan's calculation begins.
