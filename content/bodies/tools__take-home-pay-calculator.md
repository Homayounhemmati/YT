The gap between what you are offered and what arrives in your account is
consistently larger than people expect. On a $95,000 salary the difference runs to
roughly a quarter of the figure — and the exact amount depends on which state you
live in, by as much as $7,000 a year.

## How take-home pay is calculated

Four deductions come out of gross pay, in this order:

```
gross salary
  − federal income tax        (bracketed, see the breakdown below)
  − state income tax          (41 states; none in the other 9)
  − Social Security 6.2%      (up to the annual wage base)
  − Medicare 1.45%            (no ceiling; +0.9% above a threshold)
  = take-home pay
```

Social Security and Medicare together are **FICA**, 7.65% of gross for an employee.
The employer pays a matching 7.65% that never appears on the payslip. A
self-employed person pays both halves — 15.3% — which is the single largest
difference between an employee and a contractor at the same gross figure.

The wage base cap matters at higher salaries. Social Security stops applying above
an annual threshold, so the marginal deduction rate actually **falls** once you
pass it, while Medicare continues without limit.

## Why the state matters more than people assume

The federal layer is the same everywhere. The state layer is not, and it is where
the same salary produces materially different outcomes:

| On $95,000 of self-employment profit | Take-home |
|---|---|
| Alaska, Texas, Florida (no state income tax) | ~$74,160 |
| Pennsylvania (flat 3.07%) | ~$71,449 |
| Oregon (progressive, no local layer) | ~$66,993 |

**A $7,167 spread on identical gross pay**, from state tax alone. That is 7.5% of
the salary, and it is invisible in any offer letter.

An **after tax income calculator** that stops at the federal layer therefore answers the easy half of the question and leaves the half that actually varies.

## What this does not include

- Retirement contributions, health insurance premiums and HSA deductions, which
  reduce taxable income and therefore change the answer.
- Local income tax in the nine states that permit it — New York City and Maryland's
  counties are the largest cases.
- Wage garnishments, union dues and other post-tax deductions.
- Bonuses, which are withheld at a flat supplemental rate that usually differs from
  your marginal rate.

## Who needs this

Anyone comparing two offers, and anyone planning a move. Those are the two
situations where a gross figure is actively misleading rather than merely
incomplete — a higher salary in a higher-tax state can leave you with less, and a
**net pay calculator** is the only way to see it before you sign.

It is also the honest starting point for a cost-of-living comparison. What a place
costs is only half the question; what you keep there is the other half, and
comparing rent between two cities without comparing net pay answers neither.
