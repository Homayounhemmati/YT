Federal income tax is not a single rate applied to your salary. It is a series of
rates applied to slices of it, and the difference between those two ideas is the
most common misunderstanding in personal tax — the reason people believe a raise
can leave them worse off.

## How income tax is calculated

Income passes through brackets. Each rate applies only to the income inside its
own band, not to the whole amount.

```
taxable income = gross income − adjustments − the larger of
                 (standard deduction, itemised deductions)
tax = Σ (income within each bracket × that bracket's rate)
```

Two rates matter and they are not the same number:

- **Marginal rate** — the rate on your next dollar. This is the "tax bracket" people
  quote.
- **Effective rate** — total tax divided by total income. Always lower, and it is
  the number that describes what you actually paid.

Someone in the 24% bracket does not pay 24% of their income. They pay 10% on the
first slice, 12% on the next, 22% on the next, and 24% only on what sits above that
threshold. A **tax bracket calculator** that reports one number without the
breakdown hides exactly the part worth seeing.

**This is why crossing a bracket never reduces take-home pay.** Only the dollars
above the threshold are taxed at the higher rate. The belief that a raise can cost
you money is false for income tax, and it is false in every bracket.

## What a full calculation includes

- **Self-employment tax** at 15.3% for freelance income — 12.4% Social Security up
  to the annual wage base, 2.9% Medicare with no ceiling. For many self-employed
  people this exceeds their income tax.
- **State income tax**, which follows a different schedule in each of the 41 states
  that levy one, and none in the other nine.
- **The QBI deduction** under section 199A, which can remove up to 20% of qualified
  business income from taxable income before the brackets apply.

## What this does not include

- Capital gains, taxed on their own schedule at their own rates.
- Alternative Minimum Tax, which affects a small number of high earners.
- Tax credits — the Child Tax Credit, the Earned Income Credit and others reduce
  tax after it is calculated, and they can be worth more than a deduction of the
  same size.
- Local income tax, which nine states permit on top of the state rate.

Each of those moves the answer, and a calculator that silently omits them while
presenting a confident figure is worse than one that says what it left out.

## Who needs this

Anyone asking **how much tax will I pay** on a salary, a raise or a side income —
and specifically anyone who needs the answer before the money arrives rather than
after. A freelancer setting aside for a quarterly payment needs it in advance; an
employee weighing an offer needs it to compare two gross figures that are not
comparable as stated.

The bracket table below the result is the part worth reading. It shows where each
dollar went, which is the difference between a number you accept and a number you
understand.
