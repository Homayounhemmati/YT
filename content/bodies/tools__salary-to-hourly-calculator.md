An annual salary and an hourly rate describe the same pay in two currencies, and
converting between them takes one number most people get wrong: the hours actually
worked in a year.

## How an hourly rate from a salary is calculated

The standard conversion assumes a 40-hour week across all 52 weeks:

```
hourly rate = annual salary ÷ 2,080
annual salary = hourly rate × 2,080
```

**2,080 is 40 × 52.** It is the convention, and it treats paid holiday and paid
leave as worked time — which is correct for a salaried employee, because those
weeks are paid.

An **annual salary to hourly** conversion on that basis is right for anyone whose leave is paid.

It is wrong for a contractor. An unpaid two weeks of holiday and ten public
holidays takes the year to roughly 1,920 hours, and the same annual figure then
implies a rate about 8% higher. That is why a **salary to hourly wage converter**
and a contractor rate calculation are not the same tool, even though the
arithmetic looks identical.

| Basis | Hours | $100,000 becomes |
|---|---|---|
| 40 hours, 52 weeks (salaried) | 2,080 | $48.08 |
| 40 hours, 48 paid weeks (contractor) | 1,920 | $52.08 |
| 37.5 hours, 52 weeks | 1,950 | $51.28 |

## Converting in the other direction

Going from an **hourly rate to an annual salary** is the same formula reversed,
and it is the direction that matters when weighing a contract against a permanent
role.

The trap is comparing the two figures directly. A $60/hour contract is $124,800 a
year on paper, against a $110,000 salary — an apparent $14,800 advantage. It
usually is not one. The contractor pays the full 15.3% self-employment tax rather
than half of FICA, buys their own health insurance, and receives no paid leave or
employer retirement contribution. The gross figures are not comparable until those
are subtracted.

## What this does not include

- **Overtime**, which is paid at a premium and breaks the linear relationship.
- **Bonuses and commission**, which are annual but not hourly.
- **Employer benefits** — retirement matching, insurance, paid leave — which have
  real value and appear in neither figure.
- **Tax**, which differs substantially between employment and self-employment even
  at identical gross pay.

## Who needs this

Three groups, with three different questions. An employee checking whether an
hourly offer beats their salary. A contractor setting a rate that has to cover what
an employer previously paid on their behalf. And anyone comparing two offers
quoted on different bases, where the conversion is the first step and the tax
difference is the second.

For the second and third, the useful next step is a take-home calculation rather
than another gross figure — the gap between $124,800 self-employed and $110,000
salaried is much smaller after tax than before it, and occasionally the wrong way
round.
