Philadelphia's most expensive line is not the one people expect. On the federal price
measures, utilities in the Philadelphia metro area stand at 114.4 against a national
100 — higher than housing rents, at 113.1 — while goods sit below average at 96.8. The
**Philadelphia cost of living** comes out 2.6 points above average overall, but the
city also has the one tax that most changes what a job there is worth: its own wage
tax.

## A Philadelphia month

Rent at Philadelphia County's one-bedroom benchmark is $1,520, and a single person's
month, everything included, is about $3,757.

Rent is 40% of it, and the year comes to $45,078. For a couple with two children in a
two-bedroom the month is about $7,243, with rent at 25%.

## Rents, and what they include

HUD's Fair Market Rent for Philadelphia County runs from $1,397 for a studio to $2,423
for four bedrooms, and it is a gross rent: it already includes the utilities a tenant
pays. That matters here more than in most cities. Because utilities are the priciest
component of the local price level, the gap between an all-bills-included apartment and
one where the tenant pays for heat and power is wider in Philadelphia than the rent
index alone would suggest. When comparing listings, the question to ask is which bills
the rent covers.

**Average rent in Philadelphia** sits well under New York's — $1,520 for one bedroom
against $2,655 — which is the whole case for the city as a commuter base for jobs along
the corridor.

## Goods are the cheaper side

Groceries, clothing and cars cost less than the national average here, at 96.8, and
Pennsylvania levies no sales tax on groceries or on most clothing. Other services —
restaurants, health care, personal care — stand at 103.1. For a household whose budget
leans on goods rather than space and power, Philadelphia is cheaper than its overall
score.

## Pennsylvania tax, and the city's wage tax

Pennsylvania taxes wages at a flat 3.07% with no standard deduction and no personal
exemption, and it withholds a small unemployment-insurance contribution from every
paycheck. A single person on $95,000 keeps about $72,680 after federal and state tax,
an effective 23.4% — one of the better state-level results among the states checked on
this site.

Philadelphia then adds its wage tax, which is not included in any figure on this page.
It applies to everyone who lives in the city, wherever they work, and to everyone who
works in the city, wherever they live, at a lower rate for non-residents. A job in
Center City is taxed whether its holder lives in Fishtown or in the suburbs; living in
the suburbs and working outside the city avoids it entirely. That is the single largest
difference between two Philadelphia-area paychecks on the same salary.

The wage tax raises the salary a household needs here for anyone it reaches.

## Who Philadelphia suits

- **People leaving New York on a similar salary.** Rent falls by more than a third,
  goods get cheaper, and the state tax is lower than New York's — although the city wage tax gives
  some of it back to anyone who both lives and works in the city.
- **Households that can choose where the job and the home sit.** The wage tax rewards
  planning: the same salary is worth more to someone who lives in the suburbs and works
  there than to someone who does both in the city.
- **Renters who find all-inclusive leases.** They sidestep the most expensive component.

It suits least a household with heavy heating and cooling needs paying its own
utilities in an older building, where the utilities index shows up month after month.
