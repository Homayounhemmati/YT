Austin is a city where one line of the budget does almost all the work. On the
federal government's own measure, prices across the Austin metro area sit a little
below the national average overall — and rent sits far above it. Whether Austin is
expensive for you depends almost entirely on how much space you need, which is why
the **cost of living in Austin** is better read line by line than as a single score.

## Austin's month in dollars

Austin's one-bedroom benchmark is $1,562, and with ordinary spending at Austin prices a
single renter's month reaches about $3,687, 42% of it rent. Four people in two bedrooms
spend about $7,018, only 26% on rent: $1,852 buys the second bedroom, while groceries,
cars and health care more than double. Austin punishes the solo renter more than the
family.

## Rent high, everything else low

The Bureau of Economic Analysis measures prices in every metro area against a US
average of 100. Austin's overall level for 2024 is 98.1, but the components pull hard
in opposite directions:

- **Housing rents: 120.4.** Twenty points above average and the highest component
  by a wide margin.
- **Goods: 93.8.** Groceries, clothing and cars cost less than the national average.
- **Utilities: 82.0.** Electricity and water are among the cheaper parts of the
  budget.
- **Other services: 96.2.** Eating out, health care and personal services sit a
  little under average.

So is Austin expensive? For a renter, yes: housing is the component furthest from
average. For everything else, no. A buyer or renter who can hold housing costs down
— a smaller place, a longer commute, a roommate — lives in a city that is otherwise
cheaper than the national average.

Travis County studios start at $1,474 on HUD's benchmark and four bedrooms reach
$2,760, so the average rent in Austin climbs steeply only at the top of the range.

Those figures follow one of the fastest apartment-building runs of any US city. As new
towers in downtown, the Domain and along the East Riverside corridor opened, asking rents
eased from their peak — but the benchmark still sits more than twenty points above the
national level, and a household arriving now pays for the boom years' demand.

## No state income tax to set against the rent

Texas levies no personal income tax, so $95,000 keeps about $75,663 in Austin after
federal withholding, Social Security and Medicare — 20.4% gone, none of it to Texas.
Virginia would take $4,648 of that salary and New York $4,565; here, that money is
already in hand before the first Travis County lease is signed. That is the other half
of the cost to live in Austin, and it is what makes a 120.4 rent index bearable.

## Where Austin works in your favour

- **Households that need space but not much of it per person.** The step from one
  bedroom to two is small here; a couple or a family gets more for each extra dollar
  of rent than a single renter does.
- **Anyone moving from a taxed state on the same salary.** The paycheck gain is real
  and immediate, and it arrives before the rent comparison even starts.
- **People whose spending leans on goods and utilities rather than housing** — car
  owners, households with children, anyone who spends more on groceries than on
  square footage.

The loser in these numbers is the solo renter near downtown, East Austin or South
Congress: rent at a 120.4 index, paid alone, with no family discount to soften it.
