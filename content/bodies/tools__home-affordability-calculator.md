Affordability and payment are different questions, and confusing them is the most
common way buyers end up shopping in the wrong price band. A payment tool starts from a
price and returns a monthly cost. This one runs the other way: it starts from your
income and debts and returns the highest price those support.

## The 28/36 rule, and where lenders depart from it

Conventional underwriting applies two ratios to gross monthly income:

```
front-end  28%  — housing alone: principal, interest, taxes, insurance, HOA
back-end   36%  — housing plus every other monthly debt payment
```

The back-end ratio is the binding one for most buyers, because car loans, student loans
and minimum card payments all count against it while contributing nothing toward the
house. Two households with identical incomes and a $600 monthly difference in car
payments qualify for roughly $100,000 of price difference at current rates.

The 28/36 figures are guidance rather than law. The Qualified Mortgage standard allows
debt-to-income up to 43%, FHA lending routinely approves above that with compensating
factors, and automated underwriting has pushed some approvals near 50%. **How much
house can i afford** therefore has a conservative answer and a maximum answer, and the
gap between them is where most buyer regret lives.

## The four components of the payment being tested

The ratio is applied to the full housing cost, not the loan payment:

- **Principal and interest**, set by the loan amount, rate and term.
- **Property tax**, which varies from roughly 0.3% of value in Hawaii to over 2% in New
  Jersey and Illinois — a difference of more than $1,400 a month on a $1,000,000 home.
- **Homeowners insurance**, now the fastest-moving line in coastal and wildfire states.
- **Mortgage insurance**, charged whenever the down payment falls below 20% and
  cancellable on conventional loans once equity reaches that mark.

Because two of the four are set by location rather than by the loan, the same income
buys materially different houses in different states, and any **mortgage affordability
calculator** that assumes national averages for tax and insurance will be wrong in both
directions by a wide margin.

## What the ratios do not capture

Closing costs are paid at the table, not monthly, and typically run 2% to 5% of the
purchase price — money that must exist alongside the down payment rather than inside
it. Maintenance is conventionally budgeted at 1% of home value a year and appears in no
lender calculation at all. Neither reduces the price you qualify for; both reduce the
price you should want.

## Renting against buying

The affordability ceiling for buying and the ceiling for renting answer the same
underlying question about what a household can carry, and in expensive metros the two
diverge sharply. Running both before committing is the only way to see which side of
that divergence a particular city puts you on.
