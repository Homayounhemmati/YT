Most advice on this question reduces to one number: spend no more than 30% of gross
income on housing. That figure is not folklore. It comes from the Brooke Amendment,
which in 1969 capped public housing rent at 25% of tenant income, and from the 1981
revision that lifted the cap to 30%. Federal housing policy still counts a household
paying above 30% as cost burdened, and above 50% as severely cost burdened.

## Why a percentage on its own decides nothing

The rule was written for a national program, not for your metro. Thirty percent of
$70,000 is $1,750 a month, which rents a two-bedroom in Cleveland and does not rent a
studio in San Jose. A **rent calculator** that returns only a percentage of income has
told you what you may spend without telling you what that buys anywhere.

The useful version runs the comparison in both directions:

```
your ceiling      = gross monthly income × 0.30
the metro's rent  = HUD Fair Market Rent for the bedroom count you need
the answer        = whether the first number clears the second
```

HUD publishes Fair Market Rent for every county in the country, set at the 40th
percentile of rents for standard-quality units — the 50th percentile in a small number
of tight markets. It is the closest thing to an official rent figure that exists, and
it is free.

## What landlords apply instead

Screening usually runs on a gross multiple rather than a percentage. The common
threshold is three times the monthly rent in gross monthly income, which works out to
a 33% ceiling — slightly looser than the federal standard. Some markets use 2.5x, and
New York commonly uses 40x the monthly rent in annual income, a 30% ceiling expressed
annually.

This matters because the question **how much rent can i afford** has two answers that
rarely agree: what you can carry, and what a leasing office will approve you for.

## What the percentage leaves out

- Utilities. HUD's own figures come in two forms, rent alone and rent including a
  utility allowance, and the gap between them runs from $80 to over $250 a month
  depending on climate and unit type.
- Renters insurance, parking, and pet rent, none of which appear in the advertised
  figure.
- State income tax, which changes the gross-to-net ratio the whole rule is built on.
  Thirty percent of gross means something different in Oregon than in Texas.

## Where this sits in a move

Rent is the largest single line in almost every household budget and the largest
component of the regional price differences that make one city expensive and another
cheap. Working out the ceiling first, before looking at listings, is what keeps a
relocation from being decided by whichever apartment you happened to see on a Tuesday.
