This is income tax by state, side by side — a state tax rates comparison covering all
fifty states and the District of Columbia. For each it shows whether wages are taxed
at all, whether the state uses a flat rate or graduated brackets, and its top rate.
Each built state links to its paycheck calculator, where the rate is turned into what
a salary actually keeps.

## Income tax rates for all 51 jurisdictions

A flat-rate state applies one rate to all taxable income; a graduated state applies
rising rates to successive slices of it, so its top rate applies only to the income
above the top threshold. Standard deductions and personal exemptions come off before
either, which is why a state's effective rate is always below its headline rate.

## The nine states with no income tax

Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington and
Wyoming levy no broad-based income tax on wages. Their residents still pay federal
income tax, Social Security and Medicare in full, and the states raise their revenue
through sales, property and other taxes instead.

## Why the rate alone does not decide what you keep

The headline rate is the least informative number in this table. Deductions, local
income taxes in states that allow them, and the other taxes a state relies on all
change what a household keeps and spends. Compare the paycheck in each state, and then
what each place costs, before drawing a conclusion from a rate.
