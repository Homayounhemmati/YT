"Would $120,000 in Denver be a raise?" is not answerable from the number. It
depends on what you earn now, where you live now, and what both places cost — and
the answer reverses often enough that the intuition is worth distrusting.

## How an equivalent salary is calculated

The equivalent salary is the figure that leaves your standard of living unchanged
after a move:

```
equivalent salary = current salary × (destination index ÷ origin index)
```

The indices are **BEA Regional Price Parities**, which price one metro against a
national average of 100. Moving from a metro at 95 to one at 130 requires 37% more
salary to stand still — and $95,000 becomes roughly $130,000, which is a larger
number than most people guess before running it.

The comparison holds only if the two indices come from the same source and the same
year. Mixing a BEA index with an index from somewhere else produces a ratio between
two different definitions, which is arithmetic without meaning.

## Why the cost ratio is not the whole answer

An equivalent salary keeps your **spending power** level. It does not keep your
**take-home pay** level, because the two cities may sit in different states.

On a $95,000 income the gap between the highest and lowest US jurisdiction is
**$7,167 a year** in state tax alone. A move that is cost-neutral can be several
thousand dollars a year worse or better once tax is applied, and nothing in the
cost index reveals that.

So the complete comparison runs in two steps:

1. What salary keeps my spending power level? *(cost index)*
2. What does that salary actually leave me? *(tax engine)*

A **comparable salary calculator** that answers only the first is answering the
easier half of a two-part question.

## What this does not include

- Housing tenure. An owner and a renter experience the same index differently,
  because rent moves far more between metros than anything else does.
- One-off relocation costs.
- Career effects — the same job can carry different progression in different
  markets, which no index captures.
- Neighbourhood choice inside a metro, which can be worth more than the difference
  between two metros.

## Who needs this

Anyone asking **what salary do I need in** another city, and anyone negotiating a
relocation package. The equivalent figure is the strongest anchor available in that
conversation, because it is derived from published federal data rather than from a
preference — it is difficult to argue with an index and easy to argue with a
request.

It is also the right tool for a remote offer. Companies increasingly price roles by
location, and knowing the genuine equivalent tells you whether an adjustment is
reasonable or simply a reduction.
