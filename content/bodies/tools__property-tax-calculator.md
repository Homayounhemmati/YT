Property tax is the largest recurring cost of home ownership after the mortgage,
and the only one that continues after the loan is paid off. It is also the tax
people understand least, because it is calculated from a value nobody chose and a
rate expressed in units nobody uses elsewhere.

## How property tax is calculated

Three numbers, applied in order:

```
assessed value = market value × assessment ratio
taxable value  = assessed value − exemptions
annual tax     = taxable value × millage rate ÷ 1000
```

**Assessed value is not market value.** Many jurisdictions assess at a fraction of
market, and the fraction is set by state law rather than by the assessor: Georgia
assesses at 40% of fair market value, Connecticut at 70%. A $400,000 home in Georgia
has a $160,000 assessed value before anything else happens.

**A millage rate is dollars per $1,000 of taxable value.** A rate of 20 mills is 2%.
The unit exists for historical reasons and survives mainly because it makes rates
look smaller than they are, which is why a **millage rate calculator** is a
conversion tool as much as a tax tool.

**Exemptions come off before the rate applies**, and the homestead exemption for a
primary residence is the significant one. It is not automatic in most states — it
must be claimed, and people who never claim it pay more than their neighbours on
an identical house indefinitely.

## Why the effective rate is the number to compare

The rate on the tax bill is not comparable between jurisdictions, because each
applies to a differently-defined base. The comparable figure is:

```
effective rate = annual tax ÷ market value
```

Across the United States this runs from roughly 0.3% to about 2%. On a $400,000
home that is the difference between $1,200 and $8,000 a year — recurring, rising
with assessments, and more than the highest state income tax on a $95,000 salary
anywhere in the country, which is a little over $7,500 in Oregon.

**This is the trade behind "no income tax" states.** Several fund themselves
primarily through property tax, and a homeowner there can return much of the
income tax saving through the property bill. A comparison that looks at one and
not the other reaches the wrong conclusion confidently.

## What this does not include

- Special assessments for local improvements, levied separately and often
  substantial.
- Reassessment on sale, which in some states resets the assessed value to the
  purchase price — so the previous owner's bill is not a guide to yours.
- Assessment caps that limit annual increases for long-term owners, creating large
  gaps between neighbours in identical houses.
- Appeals, which succeed more often than people expect.

## Who needs this

Buyers, because **how much are property taxes** is a question best asked before the
offer rather than after the first bill. And anyone comparing two places to live,
because this is the cost most likely to reverse a comparison built on rent and
income tax alone.

Long-term owners should also check the effective rate against recent sales nearby.
An assessment that has drifted above market value is the most common and most
fixable overpayment in the whole system.
